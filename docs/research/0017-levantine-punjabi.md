Scope: new Ingredients parsley, mint, sumac, lamb, eggplant, cumin, onion, cauliflower, yogurt; existing Ingredients garlic, lemon, chile, cilantro, ginger, tomato, chicken-thigh checked against `levantine` and `punjabi`; Pairings inside the 30-Ingredient set.

Caveats that apply throughout:

- Several Levantine sources make the same dish with "beef or lamb", and Punjabi or Pakistani "mutton" usually means goat or lamb.
- The Levantine sources I read reach for allspice and cinnamon more often than cumin.
- Recipes differ by household. I note where a claim is contested.

## 2. New Ingredients

### Parsley

- **Suggested category:** herb
- **Slot types:** soft herb, garnish
- **Cuisines:**
  - levantine: Helou's tabbouleh uses 400 g flat-leaf parsley against 30 g bulgur [H2]. Wafa Shami's kafta uses parsley too [S2].
  - punjabi: not found in any Punjabi source I read. Don't tag it.
- **Essay points:**
  1. In Lebanese tabbouleh the herbs are the main ingredient, not the grain. Helou's recipe uses about 400 g parsley and 70 g mint to 30 g fine bulgur [H2], and she advises "a very small amount of grain or burghul" [H1].
  2. How you cut it matters: Helou says chopping parsley until it's "practically pulverised" bruises it, so "they will oxidise almost as soon as the dressing is added" [H1].
  3. In Palestinian kafta, parsley is chopped into ground beef or lamb with onion and spices [S2]. Shami prefers Italian (flat-leaf) parsley [S2].
  4. It's a common finishing herb, for example scattered over musakhan with pine nuts and sumac [T1], and optional over eggplant fatteh [S1].
- **Practical tip:** Wash and dry the parsley before you chop it, then keep the bunch small enough to cup your hand over and shave it into thin slivers with a sharp knife instead of rocking the blade over it [H1].
- **Cultural context:** Helou's family debated washing before or after chopping. Her mother used to chop first, then wash, which "made them mushy", and now washes first [H1].
- **Food safety:** Rinse fresh herbs under running water and don't use soap or produce wash [F2]. Keep herbs refrigerated at 40°F or below [F2].

### Mint

- **Suggested category:** herb
- **Slot types:** soft herb, garnish
- **Cuisines:**
  - levantine: tabbouleh [H2], Kassis's tomato and za'atar bread salad [K2], Assil's shish barak with mint oil [A1].
  - punjabi: Cheema's cilantro-mint raita [C3].
- **Essay points:**
  1. In tabbouleh, Helou uses mint leaves only, no stalks, cut as finely and gently as the parsley [H1][H2].
  2. Kassis's Palestinian bread salad pairs ripe tomatoes with chopped fresh mint and lemon juice [K2].
  3. Mint is a common partner for yogurt in both traditions: Assil serves lamb dumplings in yogurt sauce with mint oil [A1], and in Pakistani and Punjabi restaurants a green cilantro-mint raita often comes with kebabs, biryani and pulao [C3].
  4. In *Falastin*, fried eggplant and meatballs on hummus are "lashed with fresh mint" [T1].
- **Practical tip:** Pick the leaves off the stalks before chopping, and chop as gently as you can so they don't bruise and darken [H1][H2].
- **Cultural context:** Cheema calls green mint-cilantro raita "the condiment of choice at Indian & Pakistani restaurants" [C3]. I read that dried mint is common in Levantine cooking, but my only fetched evidence is an adapted *Falastin* kofta recipe that lists dried mint [CWB]. That's a secondary adaptation, so treat the claim as unverified (see section 5).
- **Food safety:** Same as parsley: rinse under running water and refrigerate [F2].
- **Citations:**

### Sumac

- **Suggested category:** spice. This would be a new category; the closest existing ones are "aromatic" and "citrus".
- **Slot types:** acid, garnish
- **Cuisines:**
  - levantine: musakhan [K1][T1][Y1], fattoush [EP1], mutabbal topping [D1].
  - punjabi: not found. Don't tag it.
- **Essay points:**
  1. Sumac is a red spice made from dried, crushed sumac berries, and Holden Forests & Gardens calls it a staple of Middle Eastern cooking [HFG].
  2. It's central to musakhan, which Tamimi and Wigley call "the hugely popular national dish of Palestine" [T1]. Kassis describes it as taboon bread topped with "caramelized onions cooked with copious amounts of sumac and roast chicken" [K1].
  3. Yasmin Khan describes musakhan as "pieces of chicken that you marinate in sumac, which is this astringent spice, and red onions" [Y1].
  4. It's also a finishing spice. Kassis uses 4 tablespoons to serve, on top of what's cooked into the onions and chicken [K1], and Dima Sharif sprinkles it over mutabbal [D1].
  5. Fattoush is dressed with lemon juice and sumac [EP1].
- **Practical tip:** Use sumac in two ways: cook some into the onions for depth, then sprinkle more over the dish just before serving, where its colour and sourness show most [K1].
- **Cultural context:**
  - Musakhan was traditionally made around the olive-oil pressing season in October or November [T1].
  - Kassis explains that "msakhan" means "heated", from Palestinian farmers reheating day-old taboon bread with olive oil [K1].
- **Food safety:** The cooking spice is not poison sumac. Poison sumac (*Toxicodendron vernix*) grows in bogs and swampy ground and has green drupes that turn white when ripe. As a rule of thumb, sumacs with red berries are safe and those with white berries should be avoided [HFG]. Only relevant if a Cook forages; store-bought spice is fine.
- **Citations:**
  - [K1] Reem Kassis, "Ramadan's nightly meals bring together friends, family, and nostalgia", Philadelphia Inquirer, 7 April 2022. https://www.inquirer.com/food/ramadan-recipes-reem-kassis-20220407.html
  - [T1] as above.
  - [Y1] "Palestinian cookbook celebrates a culture without a country" (Yasmin Khan interview), PBS NewsHour. https://www.pbs.org/newshour/show/palestinian-cookbook-celebrates-a-culture-without-a-country
  - [D1] Dima Sharif, "Mutabbal Beitinjan (Eggplant Mutabal / Baba Ghanouj)". https://www.dimasharif.com/recipes/eggplant-mutabal/
  - [EP1] Eat Palestine, "Fattoush" (no named author; supporting only). https://eatpalestine.com/recipes/fattoush/
  - [HFG] Holden Forests & Gardens, "Lip Sumac-ing Good: Wonderful, and Edible Sumac". https://www.holdenfg.org/blog/lip-sumac-ing-good-wonderful-and-edible-sumac/

### Lamb

- **Suggested category:** meat
- **Slot types:** protein
- **Cuisines:**
  - levantine: Wafa Shami's kafta and fatteh [S2][S1], Assil's shish barak [A1].
  - punjabi: Cheema's mutton karahi and gobi gosht [C5][C6].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Shami calls kafta with tahini "a beloved Palestinian comfort recipe made with seasoned ground beef or lamb". The meat is mixed with onion, parsley, garlic and allspice, then baked with potatoes and tahini sauce [S2].
  2. Shami prefers lamb in eggplant fatteh: "I like to use lamb, but beef can work too, or a mixture of both" [S1].
  3. In Pakistani and Punjabi cooking, "mutton" usually means young goat or lamb. Cheema notes that restaurants sometimes make traditional goat dishes with lamb [C5].
  4. Gobi gosht, meat cooked with cauliflower, is "a classic Pakistani dish", and Cheema notes that lamb is "more popular in the West" than goat [C6].
  5. Historically, meat was eaten sparingly in Punjab, "constrained by both scarcity and religious norms", though today Punjab is the least vegetarian of the three states Sidhu compares [SID].
- **Practical tip:** For karahi, finish the pan with green chillies, julienned ginger and cilantro [C5].
- **Cultural context:** Recipes in both traditions often swap lamb, goat and beef, so tag lamb as "common in", not exclusive.
- **Food safety:**
  - Cook ground lamb (kafta, kofta, meatballs) to 71°C / 160°F.
  - Whole cuts such as steaks and roasts are safe at 63°C / 145°F (medium-rare) [HC].
  - Marinate in the fridge, and never reuse a raw-meat marinade unless you boil it [AG].
- **Citations:**
  - [S1], [S2] and [A1] as above.
  - [C5] Izzah Cheema, "Mutton Karahi (Goat/Lamb Karahi)", Tea for Turmeric. https://www.teaforturmeric.com/mutton-karahi-goat-lamb-karahi/
  - [C6] Izzah Cheema, "Gobi Gosht (Goat/Lamb and Cauliflower Curry)", Tea for Turmeric. https://www.teaforturmeric.com/gobi-gosht/
  - [SID] KBS Sidhu, "The Food of Punjab Down the Ages: Gourmet Myths, Frugal Realities", The KBS Chronicle. https://kbssidhu.substack.com/p/the-food-of-punjab-down-the-ages
  - [HC] Health Canada, "Safe internal cooking temperatures". https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/general-food-safety-tips/safe-internal-cooking-temperatures.html
  - [AG] Texas A&M AgriLife Extension, "Marinade Safely", Dinner Tonight. https://dinnertonight.tamu.edu/marinade-safely/

### Eggplant

- **Suggested category:** produce
- **Slot types:** vegetable
- **Cuisines:**
  - levantine: mutabbal [D1], fatteh [S1], maqluba [AF].
  - punjabi: baingan bharta [C2][KK]. Sidhu lists aubergine (baingan) among Punjab's staples [SID].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Charring is what defines both mutabbal and baingan bharta. Sharif chars eggplants over direct heat until blackened [D1]. Kapur roasts them "over an open flame of a house burner on low setting" [KK]. Cheema notes the traditional method was roasting them over an open fire or in a tandoor [C2].
  2. The Levantine mutabbal Sharif describes is charred eggplant blended with garlic, tahini and lemon juice, topped with sumac and cumin [D1].
  3. Cheema calls baingan bharta "a prized Indian and Pakistani (Punjabi) dish". It cooks the mashed eggplant with onion, tomato, garlic, ginger, green chilli and cumin, finished with cilantro [C2].
  4. Cauliflower and eggplant are the most common vegetables in maqluba, the layered rice dish made across Syria, Jordan, Palestine and Lebanon [AF].
- **Practical tip:**
  - Poke holes in the eggplant before roasting so steam can escape [C2].
  - Cover it while it cools, which makes peeling easier [D1].
  - Mashed eggplant absorbs a lot of oil, so add a little more if the curry looks dry [C2].
- **Cultural context:** Kapur advises checking eggplants for holes, since those may harbour worms, and choosing large ones for better yield [KK].
- **Food safety:** None specific found. Ordinary produce washing applies [F2].
- **Citations:**
  - [D1], [S1] and [SID] as above.
  - [AF] Sawsan Abu Farha, "Maqluba (Palestinian one pot layered rice and vegetable torte)", Chef in Disguise. https://chefindisguise.com/2016/09/22/maqluba-palestinian-one-pot-layered-rice-and-vegetable-torte/
  - [C2] Izzah Cheema, "Easy Baingan Bharta", Tea for Turmeric. https://www.teaforturmeric.com/baingan-bharta/
  - [KK] Kunal Kapur, "Dhaba Style Baingan Ka Bharta". https://www.chefkunalkapur.com/recipe/baingan-bharta/

### Cumin

- **Suggested category:** spice (new, as for sumac)
- **Slot types:** aromatic
- **Cuisines:**
  - punjabi: strongly attested [SG][KK][C1][MG].
  - levantine: present but secondary. It's in Kassis's msakhan onions [K1], split-pea falafel [K2] and as a mutabbal topping [D1]. Several Levantine sources lean on allspice and cinnamon instead [H2][S2][AF].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Punjabi cooking often starts by sizzling whole cumin seeds in hot fat, a tadka [C1]. Singla cooks the seeds "for 40 seconds until the seeds sizzle and turn reddish-brown" before adding onion [SG].
  2. Kapur splutters cumin with whole dried red chillies before adding garlic and ginger [KK].
  3. Roasted or ground cumin seasons yogurt in Cheema's raita [C3], and ground cumin goes into her tandoori marinade "for earthiness" [C4].
  4. In Kassis's msakhan, 2 teaspoons of ground cumin cook with the sumac onions [K1].
- **Practical tip:** Let the seeds sizzle and darken a shade in the oil before adding anything wet; that's where the flavour comes from [SG].
- **Cultural context:** Cheema's Punjabi roundup describes a tadka "made of cumin seeds, red chili, and cilantro" [C1].
- **Food safety:** none.
- **Citations:**
  - [K1], [K2], [D1], [H2], [S2], [AF], [KK] and [C3] as above.
  - [SG] Anupy Singla, "Stovetop: Punjabi Aloo Gobi", Indian As Apple Pie. https://www.indianasapplepie.com/blogs/indian-as-apple-pie/aloo-gobi
  - [C1] Izzah Cheema, "50+ Traditional Punjabi Recipes", Tea for Turmeric. https://www.teaforturmeric.com/punjabi-recipes/
  - [C4] Izzah Cheema, "Tandoori Chicken", Tea for Turmeric. https://www.teaforturmeric.com/tandoori-chicken/

### Onion

- **Suggested category:** allium
- **Slot types:** aromatic, vegetable
- **Cuisines:**
  - levantine: msakhan [K1][T1], kafta [S2].
  - punjabi: aloo gobi [SG], baingan bharta [KK][C2], tandoori garnish [C4].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Musakhan is built on a large amount of onion: Kassis uses 4 large onions and ½ cup olive oil for four flatbreads [K1].
  2. Cooks differ on how far to take those onions. Kassis cooks them "until the onions have softened and cooked completely without browning, about 30 to 40 minutes" [K1]. Tamimi and Wigley take them to "completely soft and pale golden but not caramelized" [T1].
  3. In Punjabi cooking, onion usually goes in right after the cumin, often with ginger and garlic. Kapur cooks his on high heat and adds the spices "before the onion browns, and still has a bite" [KK].
  4. Raw sliced red onion is a common restaurant garnish for tandoori chicken, with lemon or lime [C4].
- **Practical tip:** For musakhan-style onions, cook low and slow, and add a splash of water or stock if they start to stick rather than letting them brown [K1].
- **Cultural context:** Onion isn't universal. Cheema leaves onion out of her karahi masala, unlike her mother's version [C5], and Gowardhan's aloo gobi has none [MG].
- **Food safety:** none.
- **Citations:** [K1], [T1], [S2], [SG], [KK], [C2], [C4], [C5] and [MG] as above.

### Cauliflower

- **Suggested category:** produce
- **Slot types:** vegetable
- **Cuisines:**
  - punjabi: aloo gobi [SG][MG][C1], gobi gosht [C6].
  - levantine: maqluba [AF][EP2], fried cauliflower with tahini [HZ], *Falastin* fritters and rice dishes [T2][T3].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Punjabi aloo gobi is cauliflower and potato cooked with cumin, turmeric, ginger and chilli [MG][SG]. Singla insists it "SHOULD BE DRY, DRY, DRY", with no tomatoes [SG].
  2. In Lebanon, fried cauliflower (arnabeet mekli) is served with tarator, a sauce of tahini, lemon juice and garlic, and warm pita [HZ].
  3. Sami Tamimi's mother made cauliflower and cumin fritters, served with mint yoghurt, for her children to take to school [T2].
  4. Cauliflower and eggplant are the most popular vegetables in maqluba [AF], which is served with yogurt or an Arabic salad [AF].
- **Practical tip:** For a dry aloo gobi, add no water. Cover the pan, cook on medium-low and stir once halfway [SG]. Gowardhan instead adds a splash of water and cooks covered [MG].
- **Cultural context:** Whether aloo gobi includes tomato is disputed. Singla calls a dry, tomato-free version "more authentically Punjabi", in her words [SG], while many published versions add tomato. Present it as a household difference.
- **Food safety:** none. Ordinary produce washing [F2].
- **Citations:**

  - [T2] Sami Tamimi and Tara Wigley, "Cauliflower and cumin fritters with mint yoghurt" (*Falastin*), ckbk. https://app.ckbk.com/recipe/fala57758c02s001r007/cauliflower-and-cumin-fritters-with-mint-yoghurt

### Yogurt

- **Suggested category:** dairy (new category)
- **Slot types:** acid, garnish. It can also act as a sauce base, which isn't an existing Slot type.
- **Cuisines:**
  - punjabi: raita [C3], tandoori marinade [C4]. Sidhu lists dahi and lassi as dairy staples [SID]. Cheema says Punjabi food "is known for its heavy use of dairy" [C1].
  - levantine: shish barak [A1], fatteh [S1], served with maqluba [AF] and musakhan [T1].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Cheema recommends thick Greek yogurt for tandoori marinade because "the thicker yogurt coats the meat better" [C4].
  2. Raita seasons yogurt with cilantro, mint, green chilli, garlic, cumin and lemon [C3].
  3. In Levantine cooking, yogurt is often heated as a sauce. Assil whisks cornstarch into the yogurt before gently simmering it for shish barak [A1].
  4. Shami's fatteh tops fried eggplant with "a garlicky tangy yogurt sauce", with lemon juice that "gives a tangy taste once mixed with yogurt" [S1].
- **Practical tip:** If you heat yogurt for a sauce, whisk in a little cornstarch first and keep it at a gentle simmer [A1].
- **Cultural context:** Sidhu describes an early-morning lassi for Punjabi field workers [SID]. Cheema serves bharta with yogurt to balance the "warming" eggplant [C2].
- **Food safety:** For yogurt-marinated chicken, marinate in the fridge [AG], cook to 74°C / 165°F [HC], and don't reuse the raw marinade unless it's boiled [AG].
- **Citations:** [C1], [C2], [C3], [C4], [SID], [A1], [S1], [AF], [T1], [AG] and [HC] as above.

### Parsley

1. **parsley + mint** (tradition, L)
   - Claim: Finely cut parsley and mint together make up most of Lebanese tabbouleh.
   - Source: [H2] lists "400 g flat-leaf parsley" and "70 g mint, leaves only" against 30 g bulgur. [H1] stresses "the importance not only of using the herbs as an ingredient, but also how important it was to chop them properly."
2. **parsley + tomato** (tradition, L)
   - Claim: Diced firm tomatoes and parsley form the body of tabbouleh.
   - Source: [H2], whose ingredient list gives 600 g firm ripe tomatoes and 400 g flat-leaf parsley against 30 g bulgur. [H1]: tomatoes are diced "into small cubes without crushing them."
3. **parsley + lemon** (contrast, L)
   - Claim: Lemon juice dresses tabbouleh and sharpens the parsley.
   - Source: [H1], "seasoning with lemon juice, extra virgin olive oil (the best you have)". [H2] lists the juice of 1 lemon.
4. **parsley + scallion** (tradition, L)
   - Claim: Helou slices spring onions into her tabbouleh.
   - Sources:
     - [H1]: "I use spring onions… I want all my ingredients to remain crisp."
     - [H2]: 50 g spring onions.
     - [EP1] (supporting): fattoush uses green onions with mint and parsley.
5. **parsley + lamb** (tradition, L)
   - Claim: Parsley is chopped into ground lamb or beef for Palestinian kafta.
   - Source: [S2], "made with seasoned ground beef or lamb" with "1 bunch fresh parsley".
6. **parsley + onion** (tradition, L)
   - Claim: Chopped onion and parsley season kafta together.
   - Source: [S2], whose kafta mixture lists 1 sweet onion, finely chopped, with "1 bunch fresh parsley" and allspice (paraphrase of the ingredient list).
7. **parsley + sumac** (contrast, L)
   - Claim: Parsley and a last sprinkle of sumac finish musakhan.
   - Source: [T1], "sprinkle with the pine nuts, parsley, remaining 1½ tsp of sumac."

### Mint
