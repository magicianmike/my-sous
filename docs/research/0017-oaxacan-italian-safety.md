# Research for issue #17: Oaxacan, Southern Italian, Ligurian, and food safety

Scope: new Ingredients tomatillo and zucchini. Existing Ingredients basil, tomato, garlic, lemon, lime, cilantro, chile, gremolata, chicken-thigh, ginger and scallion, re-checked and cited. Citations for every existing Pairing. New Pairings inside the 30-Ingredient set. Food-safety notes for lamb, pork and salmon.

## 1. Method and sources

I fetched and read every URL cited below. Quotes are as close to verbatim as the fetch tool returned them. Spanish and Italian quotes are kept in the original with a gloss, and anything I paraphrase is marked "(paraphrase)". I didn't use The Flavour Thesaurus, The Flavor Bible or any compound or flavour-molecule dataset (ADR 0001). Wikipedia and listicles helped me find dishes, but I don't cite them.

I leaned on these sources:

- **Oaxacan**
  - The *Diccionario enciclopédico de la Gastronomía Mexicana* (Larousse Cocina), the standard Mexican culinary reference, whose entries split regional Oaxacan versions by area (Valles Centrales, Mixteca, Istmo).
  - Mexico's Secretaría de Agricultura (gob.mx), including a mole verde recipe it takes from the INPI recipe book *Mulli. Oaxaca, la tierra de los moles*.
  - CONABIO (biodiversidad.gob.mx).
  - Rick Bayless's own recipe pages. He isn't from the tradition, but he names the Oaxacan versions he documents and separates them from Central Mexican ones.
- **Southern Italian**
  - Regione Campania's agriculture department (San Marzano DOP, Limone Costa d'Amalfi IGP).
  - Turismo Roma, Rome's official tourism site, which credits aglio, olio e peperoncino to Naples.
  - Neapolitan writers and sites: Portanapoli, Storie di Napoli (Imma Galluccio), Napolike (Andrea Navarro), and Ristorante Maria Grazia (the restaurant in Nerano that claims spaghetti alla Nerano).
  - Dissapore (Rossella Neri) and ITALY Magazine on pasta alla Norma.
  - Edward Behr (*The Art of Eating*) and Fine Dining Lovers on zucchine alla scapece.
- **Ligurian**
  - The Consorzio del Pesto Genovese recipe, as republished with credit on MangiareinLiguria.
  - The Consorzio di Tutela Basilico Genovese DOP.
  - Regione Liguria's Agriligurianet.
  - Slow Food (Carlo Bogliotti) on trombetta zucchini.
- **Cross-cutting technique and science**
  - America's Test Kitchen and Cook's Illustrated (named-author articles where available).
  - Oregon State's Linus Pauling Institute (garlic).
  - NMSU's Chile Pepper Institute circular.
  - Rutgers Extension (basil).
  - Eriksson et al. (cilantro genetics).
  - Lust and Paris in *Annals of Botany* (zucchini history).
  - Razifard et al. in *Molecular Biology and Evolution* (tomato domestication).
- **Cantonese checks (ginger, scallion)**
  - The Woks of Life (the Leung family).
- **Food safety**
  - US FDA (Safe Food Handling, seafood, and Food Safety for Moms-to-Be).
  - UK Food Standards Agency guidance as published on gov.uk.
  - The USDA FSIS and foodsafety.gov pages returned 403 to my fetcher, and I didn't try to get around that. I don't cite them. The FDA pages give the same federal numbers.

Caveats that apply throughout:

- In central and southern Mexico, "tomate" or "miltomate" usually means tomatillo and "jitomate" means red tomato [MX3]. Mexican "limón" is the small green *Citrus aurantifolia*, so it's a lime [L-limon].
- Oaxacan moles and salsas vary by valley and household. The Larousse entries list several regional versions, and I quote the specific one.
- Some Italian origin stories (Nerano, Norma, aglio e olio) are contested. I note the disputes below.

## 2. Ingredients

### Tomatillo (new)

- **Suggested category:** produce
- **Slot types:** acid, vegetable
- **Cuisines:**
  - oaxacan: in Oaxaca salsa verde "se prepara con ajo, chile verde y miltomate", ground in a chilmolera and mixed with chopped cilantro and onion [L-sv]. Valles Centrales mole verde contains "miltomates" [L-mv].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Tomatillo is native to Mexico. CONABIO says there's scientific evidence for Mexico as the "Centro de Diversidad y Origen de los tomates verdes" [MX2], and Purdue's NewCrop paper says it "probably was domesticated in pre-Columbian times" [P1].
  2. It grows inside a papery husk, a calyx the fruit usually splits as it grows [P1][KU1]. Its many Mexican names include "tomate verde, tomatillo, tomate de cáscara, tomate de milpa", and *miltomatl* is the Nahuatl source [MX1].
  3. It's the base of most green salsas. CONABIO: "El gran sabor de la mayoría de las salsas, no sería posible sin los tomates verdes" (most salsas' flavour wouldn't be possible without tomatillos) [MX2]. It's used to soften chile heat: "permite atenuar la pungencia… del chile" [MX2].
  4. It's acidic and tangy. Bayless: "Tomatillos are acidic and roasting adds welcome sweetness" [B-rts]. The best ones have "the most characteristic tangy, citrusy flavor" [B-tom].
  5. Before the Spanish arrived, tomatillo was used "aún más que el jitomate" (even more than red tomato) in Mesoamerican cooking [MX2].
- **Practical tip:** Husk and rinse before cooking, since every Bayless recipe calls for tomatillos "husked and rinsed" [B-rts]. Pick "light lime-green tomatillos that are firm and completely fill the papery husk", and keep them "in the refrigerator, loosely covered, for a month or more" [B-tom].
- **Cultural context:** Tomatillo isn't an unripe red tomato. Mexico's agriculture ministry calls them "dos productos diferentes" from different plants [MX3]. In northern Mexico "tomate" can mean either, so the names cause confusion [MX3].
- **Citations:** [MX1], [MX2], [MX3], [P1], [KU1], [B-rts], [B-tom], [L-sv], [L-mv] (see section 7).

### Zucchini (new)

- **Suggested category:** produce
- **Slot types:** vegetable
- **Cuisines:**
  - southern-italian: spaghetti alla Nerano is from Nerano on the Sorrento peninsula, made with "Zucchine fritte, scarti di formaggio e basilico" [SN1]. Zucchine alla scapece is Neapolitan [AE1][FDL1].
  - ligurian: trombetta zucchini are "onnipresenti nella cucina ligure" and go into savoury tarts, frittate and stuffed vegetables [SF1][SF2]. Caveat: trombetta is a different squash from standard zucchini, so tag ligurian with care.
  - levantine: in kousa bil laban, Lebanese zucchini are stuffed with rice and lamb or beef and simmered in garlic yogurt [HZ1]. Outside this file's Cuisines; see Pairings.
- **Essay points:**
  1. Zucchini is a young Italian cultivar group of an American plant, *Cucurbita pepo*. Lust and Paris trace the Zucchini Group to near Milan, "perhaps as early as 1850" (paraphrase of abstract) [EP1].
  2. In Naples, zucchine alla scapece are fried, then marinated "for a short time in vinegar" with mint and often garlic. The name likely comes from Spanish *escabeche* [AE1].
  3. In spaghetti alla Nerano the zucchini are fried first, and cheese and pasta water make the sauce creamy without cream [NL1].
  4. Ligurian trombette are picked very young, with flesh "molto tenera e compatta" (very tender and firm) and less watery than other courgettes [SF1].
- **Practical tip:** For frying, slice thin (3–4 mm), dry the slices well and fry a few at a time so they don't turn greasy [FDL1].
- **Cultural context:** The Nerano dish is a 20th-century invention, not an old one. The restaurant credits Donna Rosa in 1952 [MG1], and Napolike notes the original cheese was leftover local caciotta or pecorino, not Provolone del Monaco [NL1]. Attribution between Maria Grazia and her daughter Rosa varies by source [MG1][SN1].
- **Citations:** [EP1], [AE1], [FDL1], [SN1], [NL1], [MG1], [SF1], [SF2], [HZ1].

### Basil (existing)

- **Current file:** category herb, cuisines southern-italian and ligurian, slot soft herb. Tip: "Tear or slice it right before serving; cut basil darkens quickly." Essay: aroma fragile, fades with long cooking; sweet basil most used in Italian cooking.
- **Suggested category / slots:** keep herb / soft herb.
- **Cuisines:**
  - ligurian: Basilico Genovese DOP is the "ingrediente base nella ricetta del pesto genovese" [AL1]. The DOP consortium calls it "il prodotto simbolo della Liguria" [BG1].
  - southern-italian: in pasta alla Norma, "basil should never be left out" (paraphrase) [IM1]. Spaghetti alla Nerano uses basil [SN1]. San Marzano DOP rules allow basil leaves in the tin [RC1].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Basil became a traditional crop in Genoa "a partire dal XIX secolo", after greenhouses arrived [AL1].
  2. The Consorzio's pesto recipe tells you to tear the leaves gently, not cut them ("stracciare, non tranciare"), and to work quickly to avoid oxidation (paraphrase) [CP1].
  3. Basil is chilling-sensitive. Rutgers lists "blackening of leaves, stem discoloration, and loss of aroma" as symptoms and gives 55°F as the best storage temperature [RU1].
  4. Neapolitan cooks often split it: Portanapoli's tomato spaghetti puts half the basil in the sauce and adds the rest at the end, "spezzettate con le mani" (torn by hand) [PN3].
- **Practical tip (revised):** Keep basil out of the fridge (it blackens below about 55°F / 13°C) [RU1], and tear it by hand just before serving [CP1][PN3].
- **Cultural context:** The pesto consortium sets the garlic at "uno spicchio d'aglio ogni trenta foglie di basilico" (one clove per thirty leaves) [CP1].
- **Flags:**
  - "Cut basil darkens quickly": I didn't find a fetched source on cut versus torn darkening. The Consorzio supports tearing and speed, and Rutgers supports cold-blackening. Reword around those.
  - "Sweet basil is the type most often used in Italian cooking": unverified. I'd swap it for the Genovese DOP fact.
  - "Aroma fades with long cooking": not directly sourced. The half-in, half-at-the-end practice [PN3] implies it but doesn't state it.
- **Citations:** [AL1], [BG1], [CP1], [RU1], [PN3], [IM1], [SN1], [RC1].

### Tomato (existing)

- **Current file:** produce, southern-italian and oaxacan, slot vegetable. Tip: salt cut tomatoes to draw juice. Essay: sweetness, acidity, savoury depth; canned whole better out of season.
- **Suggested category / slots:** keep produce / vegetable (also acid is defensible, but I didn't find a source framing it that way).
- **Cuisines:**
  - southern-italian: San Marzano DOP, prized as "il rosso sugo che copriva il bianco della pasta di Gragnano" (the red sauce over Gragnano pasta) [RC1]. Pasta alla Norma [DS1].
  - oaxacan: mole coloradito, one of the "siete moles oaxaqueños", contains "jitomate, ajo, ajonjolí, cebolla" [L-mc]. Bayless's Oaxacan pork picadillo uses tomato [B-pic].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Tomato's domestication finished in Mesoamerica. A 2020 genomic study describes a step from wild-like cherry tomato in South America to fully domesticated tomato "in Mesoamerica" [MBE1]. Larousse calls it American in origin and widely grown through Mesoamerica before the Spanish [L-jit].
  2. The Mexican word *jitomate* is from Nahuatl *xitomatl*, "tomate con ombligo" (tomato with a navel), for the stem scar [L-jit].
  3. San Marzano has a "sapore tipicamente agrodolce" (sweet-sour flavour). The DOP covers peeled tomatoes ("pelato"), whose processing brings out its qualities [RC1].
  4. ATK calls canned whole tomatoes "reliably sweet year-round" [ATK-tom].
- **Practical tip:** Keep the current tip only if another source backs it; I couldn't find a fetched source for salting to draw juice. Alternative with a source: use canned whole tomatoes when fresh ones are pale [ATK-tom].
- **Cultural context:** In Oaxaca and central Mexico, "tomate" on a menu likely means tomatillo; red tomato is "jitomate" [MX3].
- **Flags:**
  - The salting tip is unsupported by my sources.
  - "Savoury depth" isn't sourced.
- **Citations:** [MBE1], [L-jit], [RC1], [ATK-tom], [L-mc], [B-pic], [DS1], [MX3].

### Garlic (existing)

- **Current file:** allium; southern-italian, ligurian, cantonese, oaxacan; aromatic. Tip: cook gently, bitter if browned. Essay: raw sharp, slow cooking sweet; crushed stronger than sliced.
- **Cuisines:**
  - southern-italian: aglio, olio e peperoncino is "originaria della Campania in generale e del napoletano in particolare" [TR1].
  - ligurian: Consorzio pesto uses two cloves for 50 g basil [CP1].
  - oaxacan: Oaxacan salsa verde ("ajo, chile verde y miltomate") [L-sv]. Cecina oaxaqueña marinade [L-cec].
  - cantonese: The Woks of Life cook ginger and garlic briefly in oil before greens [WL2]. That page calls itself Chinese restaurant-style, not specifically Cantonese, so a better Cantonese-specific source is still wanted.
- **Essay points (all supported):**
  1. Crushing or chopping releases alliinase, and allicin forms "within 10 to 60 seconds". Intact cloves contain none [LPI1].
  2. "The more you slice, chop, or crush garlic the more allicin is produced and the more pungent the garlic will be." Whole cloves are mildest, then sliced, minced and paste [ATK-g1].
  3. Garlic's enzymes are destroyed above 140°F, "This is why toasted or roasted garlic has a mellow, slightly sweet flavor" [ATK-g2].
  4. "Garlic browned (or overbrowned) at very high temperatures (300 to 350 degrees) results in a bitter flavor" [ATK-g2].
- **Practical tip:** The current one is supported [ATK-g2][TR1]. Turismo Roma: "l'aglio deve leggermente dorare e non bruciare" (garlic should colour slightly, not burn) [TR1].
- **Cultural context:** The pesto consortium wants garlic "dolce" and in the background (paraphrase), one clove per thirty leaves [CP1].
- **Flags:** none on the essay. The cantonese tag needs a Cantonese-labelled source.
- **Citations:** [LPI1], [ATK-g1], [ATK-g2], [TR1], [CP1], [L-sv], [L-cec], [WL2].

### Lemon (existing)

- **Current file:** citrus, southern-italian, acid. Tip: zest before juicing, zest carries aroma. Essay: brightness at end; juice is acidity, zest is oils.
- **Cuisines:**
  - southern-italian: Limone Costa d'Amalfi IGP is grown on terraces of the Amalfi coast [RC2].
- **Essay points:**
  1. The Amalfi lemon's peel has an intense scent that Regione Campania puts down to its "ricchezza di oli essenziali e terpeni" (wealth of essential oils and terpenes) [RC2]. That supports "zest carries aroma".
  2. Locally it's used with fish, seafood starters, pasta and meat, as well as limoncello and desserts (paraphrase) [RC2].
  3. History: "Già nell'XI secolo, la Repubblica Amalfitana decretò" that ships carry lemons against scurvy (paraphrase of rest) [RC2].
  4. Gremolata uses lemon zest raw, at the end, over braised meat [MF1][SDG1].
- **Practical tip:** The current one is supported in spirit by [RC2]. "Zest before you juice" is common sense but I found no source for it.
- **Cultural context:** Lemons reached Campania by way of the Arabs in Spain and Sicily (paraphrase) [RC2].
- **Flags:** "Adds brightness, often at the end of cooking" is supported only indirectly [ATK-lb][MF1].
- **Citations:** [RC2], [MF1], [SDG1], [ATK-lb].

### Lime (existing)

- **Current file:** citrus, oaxacan, acid. Tip: add at end. Essay: "sharper and more floral than lemon"; squeeze at table lifts rich or spicy food.
- **Cuisines:**
  - oaxacan: Larousse lists Oaxaca among the states that produce Mexican limón [L-limon]. Chile de agua is served as "rajas curtidas en limón" (strips cured in lime) over mole amarillo or chichilo [L-cda]. Cecina oaxaqueña is served in the Valles Centrales with lime, grilled green onions and radishes (paraphrase) [L-cec].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Mexican limón is *Citrus aurantifolia*, "de sabor agrio y piel color verde". The small "limón chico" has a very acid taste (paraphrase) [L-limon].
  2. It's "indispensable para la elaboración del cebiche" [L-limon].
  3. Mixed with salt and chile powder, it goes "sobre cualquier verdura o fruta" (on any vegetable or fruit) [L-limon].
  4. At the table: Bayless serves his oregano chicken thighs "with salsa and lime wedges to squeeze on" [B-oreg].
- **Practical tip:** "Add at the end" fits the at-the-table usage [B-oreg][L-cec], but I found no source on cooking dulling lime.
- **Flags:**
  - "Sharper and more floral than lemon" is unsupported. Drop it or find a source.
  - Note in the content that Mexican recipes saying "limón" mean lime.
- **Citations:** [L-limon], [L-cda], [L-cec], [B-oreg].

### Cilantro (existing)

- **Current file:** herb, oaxacan and cantonese, soft herb. Tip: use the tender stems. Essay: citrusy; some find it soapy.
- **Cuisines:**
  - oaxacan: Oaxacan salsa verde mixes in chopped cilantro [L-sv]. The pork version of mole amarillo "suele contener cilantro" [L-ma]. The Mixteca (Yucanama) mole verde uses cilantro [L-mv].
  - cantonese: not verified by me; see the Cantonese research file.
- **Essay points:**
  1. Cilantro "fue traído a México por los españoles" (was brought to Mexico by the Spanish) and has been widely used since [L-cil].
  2. It's chopped into "un sinnúmero de salsas cocidas y crudas" (countless cooked and raw salsas) and used as a garnish on sopes, chilaquiles and ceviches [L-cil].
  3. The soapy taste has a genetic link. A 23andMe study found a variant near olfactory receptor genes, including OR6A2, which "has a high binding specificity for several of the aldehydes" in cilantro [AR1].
- **Practical tip:** The current one is supported: "Suele utilizarse casi toda la rama, debido a que tiene mucho sabor" (usually almost the whole sprig is used, because it has a lot of flavour) [L-cil].
- **Cultural context:** Cilantro arrived with the Spanish, so it isn't pre-Hispanic in Mexico [L-cil]. Bayless notes cilantro and parsley were later additions to green mole [B-gm].
- **Flags:** "Bright, citrusy aroma" isn't sourced. The Eriksson paper says "soapy", and the current wording is fine.
- **Citations:** [L-cil], [L-sv], [L-ma], [L-mv], [AR1], [B-gm].

### Chile (existing)

- **Current file:** produce; southern-italian, oaxacan; heat. Tip: taste first, heat varies. Essay: heat and green fruity flavour; heat in pale ribs.
- **Cuisines:**
  - southern-italian: "Peperoncino piccante (preferibilmente fresco)" in aglio, olio e peperoncino [TR1].
  - oaxacan: chile de agua "se utiliza principalmente en Oaxaca"; it's "muy picoso" and roasted and peeled like poblanos [L-cda].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Capsaicinoids are "produced in glands or vesicles on the fruit's placenta". Seeds "are not the source of heat", though they can pick some up [NM1].
  2. "Genetics, weather, growing conditions, and fruit age affect capsaicinoid content and thus the heat level" [NM1]. That supports the tip.
  3. Portanapoli says fresh chile has a stronger aroma than dried in aglio e olio, and some cooks use black pepper instead (paraphrase) [PN1].
- **Flags:**
  - "Much of the heat sits in the pale ribs": mostly right, but the source tissue is the placenta, the pale core the seeds hang from (paraphrase) [NM1]. Suggested wording: "Most of the heat is made in the pale core and ribs the seeds cling to, so cutting those away tames it."
  - "Green, fruity flavour" is unsourced.
- **Citations:** [NM1], [TR1], [L-cda], [PN1].

### Gremolata (existing)

- **Current file:** blend of lemon, garlic, parsley; garnish. Cultural context: served with osso buco of Milan.
- **Cuisines:** Milanese/Lombard. None of the eight Cuisines fit, so keep it untagged.
- **Essay points:**
  1. Gremolada is "buccia di limone, aglio, prezzemolo e a volte acciuga, tritati finemente" (lemon zest, garlic, parsley and sometimes anchovy, finely chopped) [SDG1].
  2. It goes over ossobuco "appena prima di servire" (just before serving), or in the last 2–3 minutes for a more blended flavour (paraphrase) [MF1].
  3. The Comune di Milano gave ossobuco alla milanese a De.Co. on 14 December 2007 [MF1].
  4. The lemon-zest preparations of this kind date to the second half of the 1700s, per a source quoted by D'Alma [SDG1].
- **Practical tip:** The current one is supported in practice by [MF1]. The claim that the zest and garlic "fade as it sits" isn't sourced.
- **Flags:** Anchovy is a common variant [SDG1], so maybe mention it. Cultural context is supported [MF1].
- **Citations:** [SDG1], [MF1].

### Chicken thigh (existing)

- **Current file:** meat, protein. Tip: start skin-side down in a medium pan. Essay: more fat and connective tissue, forgiving; bone-in skin-on most flavour. USDA safety citations.
- **Cuisines (optional):**
  - oaxacan: "It's common in Oaxaca to poach chicken to serve with this mole" (black mole) [B-bm]. The Mixteca mole verde "suele ser de pollo" [L-mv].
  - cantonese: bak chit gai, poached chicken [WL1].
  - Both are whole-bird dishes, not thigh-specific.
- **Essay points:**
  1. ATK: "The fat and collagen in the thighs will keep them tender and moist", and it's "fine if the internal temp of the chicken rises above 175 degrees" [ATK-ct2].
  2. Starting bone-in, skin-on thighs in a cold skillet means "the fat slowly renders until the skin turns golden brown and crispy". Cook them to at least 175°F for tenderness (paraphrase) [ATK-ct1].
- **Flags:**
  - The tip says "medium pan". ATK recommends starting cold [ATK-ct1], so consider "start skin-side down in a cold pan".
  - "Bone-in, skin-on thighs bring the most flavour" is unsourced.
  - The safety block's USDA URLs couldn't be re-fetched (403). Equivalent FDA numbers: poultry 165°F (74°C) [FDA3].
- **Citations:** [ATK-ct1], [ATK-ct2], [B-bm], [L-mv], [WL1], [FDA3].

### Ginger (existing)

- **Current file:** aromatic, cantonese. Tip: scrape skin off with a spoon. Essay: grated melts into sauces; sliced flavours broth and is lifted out.
- **Cuisines:**
  - cantonese: raw ginger scallion oil "represents the epitome of Cantonese cooking" [WL3]. Bak chit gai is poached with "2 scallions and 5 slices of ginger" [WL1].
- **Essay points:**
  1. Sliced ginger goes into the poaching water for Cantonese white-cut chicken [WL1].
  2. Ginger scallion oil is served with poached chicken and Cantonese roast meats [WL3][WL4].
  3. Ginger burns fast in oil. Woks of Life starts it over low heat and warns "Burning them can create bitterness" [WL2].
- **Practical tip:** Supported: "A curved spoon is much easier to maneuver around the root, making it easy to remove the peel" (Megan Ginsberg, ATK) [ATK-gin].
- **Flags:** "Clean, warming heat" isn't sourced. "Grated melts into sauces" isn't directly sourced either, though ATK says grate with a rasp [ATK-gin].
- **Citations:** [WL1], [WL2], [WL3], [WL4], [ATK-gin].

### Scallion (existing)

- **Current file:** allium, cantonese, aromatic and garnish. Tip: cook whites, scatter greens. Essay: milder than onions; whites cook like gentle onion, greens grassy.
- **Cuisines:**
  - cantonese: ginger scallion oil [WL3].
  - oaxacan: cecina oaxaqueña is served in the Valles Centrales with grilled green onions [L-cec]. Larousse also describes *cebollitas cambray asadas*, sometimes with lime juice, as a taco garnish [L-ceb]; that entry isn't Oaxaca-specific.
- **Essay points:**
  1. In Cantonese ginger scallion oil, scallion and ginger are minced raw with oil and salt. Some cooks pour hot oil over them [WL3][WL4].
  2. Grilled green onions are a common Mexican taco-stand garnish, cooked "solas o rociadas con jugo de limón, aceite y sal" (plain or with lime juice, oil and salt) [L-ceb].
- **Flags:**
  - "Milder than onions" and the whites/greens split are unsourced in what I fetched.
  - The tip is reasonable but uncited.
- **Citations:** [WL3], [WL4], [L-cec], [L-ceb].

## 5. Food safety: lamb, pork, salmon

These are in the shape of `chicken-thigh.md` (a `handling` note and a `cooking` note, each with citations). Where the US and UK differ, I give the more conservative choice and note the other. Fahrenheit and Celsius pairs are as published by FDA, except 40°F → 4°C, which is my conversion.

### Lamb (whole cuts and ground)

- **handling:**
  - Note: Keep raw lamb covered and away from ready-to-eat food in the fridge (40°F / 4°C or below), and refrigerate it within 2 hours of buying. Don't rinse it, since washing doesn't remove bacteria and can spread them. Use a separate board, or prepare vegetables first and the raw meat last. Wash your hands with soap and warm water for at least 20 seconds after handling it.
  - Citations:
    - FDA, "Safe Food Handling": "for at least 20 seconds"; "Refrigerate or freeze meat, poultry, eggs, seafood, and other perishables within 2 hours"; fridge "consistently 40° F or below". https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/safe-food-handling
    - Food Standards Agency, "Why avoiding cross-contamination is important" (gov.uk): "Washing meat, fish and poultry doesn't get rid of harmful bacteria - only thorough cooking will"; "prepare vegetables and fruit first followed by raw meat, fish or poultry last"; "Always wash hands after handling raw meat, fish and poultry." https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/why-avoiding-cross-contamination-is-important/why-avoiding-cross-contamination-is-important
- **cooking:**
  - Note: For chops, steaks and roasts, cook until a thermometer reads at least 145°F / 63°C, then rest 3 minutes. The UK allows whole cuts pink in the middle once the outside is seared. Cook ground lamb (kofta, burgers) and rolled or skewered joints all the way through, to 160°F / 71°C, since mincing moves surface bacteria inside.
  - Citations:
    - FDA, "Meat, Poultry & Seafood (Food Safety for Moms-to-Be)": "Cook beef, pork, veal, and lamb roasts, steaks, and chops to at least 145° F (63° C), with a 3 minute rest time"; "Cook ground beef, veal, lamb, and pork to at least 160° F (71° C)." https://www.fda.gov/food/people-risk-foodborne-illness/meat-poultry-seafood-food-safety-moms-be
    - FDA, "Safe Food Handling" chart: "Beef, Pork, Veal, and Lamb" (chops, roasts, steaks) "145oF with a 3 minute rest time"; "Ground Meat" "160oF". https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/safe-food-handling
    - Food Standards Agency, "Cooking your food" (gov.uk): whole cuts of beef or lamb "can be served pink in the middle according to taste"; "When a whole cut of meat is minced or skewered, this moves any bacteria from the outside all the way through the meat"; middle should reach "70°C for 2 minutes" or an equivalent time-temperature. https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cooking-your-food/cooking-your-food

### Pork

- **handling:**
  - Note: Same as lamb. Refrigerate within 2 hours at 40°F / 4°C or below, keep it covered and apart from ready-to-eat food, don't rinse it, and wash hands and boards after.
  - Citations: the FDA "Safe Food Handling" and FSA cross-contamination pages above.
- **cooking:**
  - Note: Cook chops, roasts and tenderloin to at least 145°F / 63°C on a thermometer and rest 3 minutes. Cook ground pork and sausage to 160°F / 71°C. The UK is stricter on whole cuts: it advises cooking pork until there's no pink meat, because bacteria can be all the way through. If you want one rule, 70°C for 2 minutes in the centre meets both.
  - Citations:
    - FDA, "Food Safety for Moms-to-Be": quotes as above (pork in both the 145°F whole-cut and 160°F ground lines). https://www.fda.gov/food/people-risk-foodborne-illness/meat-poultry-seafood-food-safety-moms-be
    - FDA, "Safe Food Handling": "Ham, uncooked" (fresh or smoked) "145oF with a 3 minute rest time". https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/safe-food-handling
    - Food Standards Agency, "Cooking your food": "Poultry … and pork can have bacteria all the way through the meat"; check for "no pink, fleshy meat". https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cooking-your-food/cooking-your-food
  - Open choice for the author: the US allows 145°F with a rest, which can look slightly pink, but the UK says no pink. I'd lead with the FDA number and mention the UK view, or use 70°C / 158°F for 2 minutes to meet both.
  - Oaxacan cecina: thin and marinated, it should still be cooked through before eating (no extra source; it's covered by the whole-cut and UK pork guidance).

### Salmon and other fish

- **handling:**
  - Note: Keep fresh fish in the fridge at 40°F / 4°C or below and use it within 2 days, or wrap it tightly and freeze it. Thaw in the fridge overnight, or sealed in a bag in cold water. Wash boards, utensils and counters with soap and hot water between raw fish and ready-to-eat food. Don't rinse raw fish.
  - Citations:
    - FDA, "Selecting and Serving Fresh and Frozen Seafood Safely": store "at a temperature of 40°F or below" if used within 2 days; thaw "gradually by placing it in the refrigerator overnight" or "seal it in a plastic bag and immerse it in cold water"; wash "cutting boards, dishes, utensils, and counter tops with soap and hot water". https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/selecting-and-serving-fresh-and-frozen-seafood-safely
    - FSA cross-contamination page (no washing of fish), above.
- **cooking:**
  - Note: Cook fish to 145°F / 63°C, or until the flesh is opaque and separates easily with a fork. Neither FDA nor FSA gives a rest time for fish. Raw or rare salmon (sashimi, crudo) is a risk: some fish carry parasites, and freezing kills those but not all germs. Pregnant people, young children, older adults and anyone with a weakened immune system should avoid raw or undercooked fish.
  - Citations:
    - FDA, "Selecting and Serving Fresh and Frozen Seafood Safely": "Most seafood should be cooked to an internal temperature of 145°F"; "The flesh is clear and separates easily with a fork"; "Some species of fish can contain parasites"; "freezing doesn't kill all harmful germs"; at-risk groups: "Pregnant women," "Children," "Older adults," "Persons with weakened immune systems". https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/selecting-and-serving-fresh-and-frozen-seafood-safely
    - FDA, "Food Safety for Moms-to-Be": "Finfish should be cooked to an internal temperature of 145° F (63° C)." https://www.fda.gov/food/people-risk-foodborne-illness/meat-poultry-seafood-food-safety-moms-be
    - Food Standards Agency, "Cooking your food": "fish flesh should turn opaque (no longer transparent) and separates easily with a fork". https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/cooking-your-food/cooking-your-food
  - Note: the FDA doesn't give a home-freezing time and temperature for parasite control on these pages, so the app shouldn't suggest home-freezing makes salmon safe raw.
