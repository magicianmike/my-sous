## 1. Method and sources

Every URL below was fetched and read during this research, and each one supports the claim it's attached to. Quotes are verbatim or a close paraphrase of what the page said. I didn't use any pairing books or compound datasets (ADR 0001), and I used Wikipedia only to find sources.

I relied most on these sources:

- **Fuchsia Dunlop** for Sichuan. She trained as a chef at the Sichuan Institute of Higher Cuisine, and these are her recipes reprinted with credit by named publishers: SBS Food, NPR/VPM (from *Land of Plenty*), The Splendid Table (from *Every Grain of Rice*), The Caterer, plus interviews in The National and The Irish News. Sous Chef (a UK retailer's blog) reprints one of her recipes with credit to *The Food of Sichuan*. That's weaker as a host, but the recipe text is hers.
- **The Woks of Life** (the Leung family, Chinese-American; Bill Leung's recipes come from his father's Cantonese restaurant) for Cantonese, and for some Sichuan ingredient background.
- **Made With Lau** (Daddy Lau, a Cantonese chef with 60+ years' experience, and his son Randy) for Cantonese stir-fry technique.
- **Tony Tan in Gourmet Traveller** (a named chef-author) for Cantonese sweet and sour pork.
- **Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries (MAFF)**: "Our Regional Cuisines", "Traditional Foods in Japan" and the Japanese-only にっぽん伝統食図鑑 pages. I translated the Japanese pages, and the translations are mine.
- **Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook** (a Japanese home cook) for Japanese home dishes, and **Yumiko, RecipeTin Japan** (born and raised in Japan) for yaki nasu.
- **FDA** and **CDC** for food safety.

Caveat: the Japanese side leans heavily on one writer (Just One Cookbook). MAFF backs up the miso, rice vinegar and salmon claims, but the shiso and pork claims rest on Just One Cookbook alone.

## 2. New Ingredients

### sichuan-pepper

- **Suggested category:** spice
- **Slot types:** heat, garnish. Its "heat" is a numbing tingle, not chile burn, so the essay should make that clear.
- **Cuisines:** sichuan. Sources: The Woks of Life (Sichuan peppercorns); The National (Dunlop).
  - Japanese cooking uses *sansho*, a related prickly-ash pepper. For example, MAFF's Ishikari nabe page says the hot pot is "sprinkled with sansho (Japanese pepper) as an accent at the end." Sansho isn't the same product, so I wouldn't tag sichuan-pepper as Japanese.
- **Factual points (essay):**
  1. The spice is the dried berry husk of the prickly-ash tree, and it isn't related to black pepper. The Woks of Life: "the reddish-pink berries of the prickly-ash tree… they have no relation to the peppercorns in a grinder on your kitchen table!"
  2. In larger amounts it gives "a pleasant numbing sensation on the tongue and lips." Combined with chillies it makes *mala*, "numbing-spicy" (The Woks of Life). The National describes "a zingy tingliness that lasts for several minutes."
  3. It is native to Sichuan, while chilli came later. The National: "Unlike the chilli, which only arrived in China in the 16th century… the Sichuan pepper is native to the mountain slopes of the province."
  4. Dunlop, quoted in The National: "In many Sichuanese households the pepper is roasted and freshly ground almost every day."
- **Practical tip:** Pick out stems and the black seeds, which taste gritty, then toast the husks lightly before grinding them in a mortar or spice grinder (The Woks of Life). Ground pepper loses aroma, so grind small batches.
- **Cultural context:** It is half of mala, the numbing-and-hot style. Dunlop pushes back on the idea that Sichuan food is only heat: "Good Chinese and Sichuan food is all about balance, not about battering your palate with lots of heat" (Irish News).
- **Food safety:** None specific. The Woks of Life notes that US imports were banned for decades over citrus canker, and the ban was partially lifted in 2004. That was a plant-health rule, not a food-safety one.
- **Citations:**
  - Sichuan Peppercorns, The Woks of Life. https://thewoksoflife.com/sichuan-peppercorns/
  - "'The Food of Sichuan': how a British writer brought the flavours of provincial China to the world", Chandrahas Choudhury, The National (2 June 2020). https://www.thenationalnews.com/lifestyle/food/the-food-of-sichuan-how-a-british-writer-brought-the-flavours-of-provincial-china-to-the-world-1.1028035
  - "Fuchsia Dunlop: Sichuan food is all about balance, not battering your palate with heat", Ella Walker, The Irish News (9 Nov 2019). https://www.irishnews.com/lifestyle/2019/11/09/news/fuchsia-dunlop-sichuan-food-is-all-about-balance-not-battering-your-palate-with-heat-1758271/
  - Ishikarinabe (Ishikari hot pot), Our Regional Cuisines, MAFF. https://www.maff.go.jp/e/policies/market/k_ryouri/search_menu/565/index.html

### pork

- **Suggested category:** meat
- **Slot types:** protein
- **Cuisines:**
  - sichuan: twice-cooked pork (Dunlop, *Land of Plenty*, via NPR/VPM).
  - japanese: shogayaki and tonjiru (Just One Cookbook).
  - cantonese: char siu (The Woks of Life) and sweet and sour pork (Tony Tan, Gourmet Traveller).
- **Factual points (essay):**
  1. Sichuan twice-cooked pork boils a fatty cut, then slices and stir-fries it with chilli bean paste. Dunlop suggests pork belly as being "of a similar character" to the Sichuan cut (NPR/VPM).
  2. In Japan, shogayaki (ginger pork) is "a homey Japanese dish" and "popular mom-style food at home", usually made with thin pork loin (Just One Cookbook).
  3. Cantonese char siu is "a type of Cantonese roast meat", made from boneless pork shoulder or butt with "some good fat on it" (The Woks of Life).
  4. Fat matters across all three: The Woks of Life says not to trim char siu ("it will render off and add flavor"), and tonjiru uses pork belly for a rich broth.
- **Practical tip:** For shogayaki, "make several slits on the connective tissue (white area) between the meat and fat" so the slices don't curl (Just One Cookbook).
- **Cultural context:** For many Sichuanese people abroad, twice-cooked pork is a dish of nostalgia (NPR/VPM). Tony Tan writes that sweet and sour pork "is a classic Cantonese dish" and part of many people's childhoods.
- **Food safety:** Cook whole cuts of pork to 145°F / 63°C, "then allow the meat to rest for 3 minutes before carving or eating." Cook ground pork to 160°F / 71°C (CDC). The Woks of Life's char siu recipe also points to the USDA's 145°F-plus-rest figure.
- **Citations:**
  - Twice-Cooked Pork (Fuchsia Dunlop, from *Land of Plenty*), NPR via VPM. https://www.vpm.org/npr-news/2008-05-02/twice-cooked-pork
  - Ginger Pork (Shogayaki), Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook. https://www.justonecookbook.com/ginger-pork-shogayaki/
  - Tonjiru, Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook. https://www.justonecookbook.com/tonjiru/
  - Chinese BBQ Pork (Char Siu), Bill Leung, The Woks of Life. https://thewoksoflife.com/chinese-bbq-pork-cha-siu/
  - Sweet and sour pork, Tony Tan, Gourmet Traveller. https://www.gourmettraveller.com.au/recipe/mains/sweet-and-sour-pork-14234/
  - Preventing Food Poisoning, CDC. https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/prevention/index.html

### doubanjiang

- **Suggested category:** condiment (fermented paste)
- **Slot types:** heat. It also seasons the base, so it could act as an aromatic, but it's closest to heat. There's no "savoury/umami" slot type; see section 5.
- **Cuisines:** sichuan. Sources: Dunlop (mapo tofu, twice-cooked pork, fish-fragrant aubergines); The Woks of Life.
- **Factual points (essay):**
  1. It is "a paste made from fermented soybeans, broad beans, and chilies"; the best-known kind, Pixian, is "named after the town of Pixian in Sichuan province" (The Woks of Life). The National describes it as "made with fermented broad beans."
  2. Frying it in oil is what gives many Sichuan dishes their red colour: "Once the sauce hits the oil, it immediately begins to turn red!" (The Woks of Life). Dunlop's mapo tofu: "fry the chilli bean sauce until the oil turns red" (SBS).
  3. It is "quite salty", so cut back on soy sauce when using it (The Woks of Life).
  4. It is the base of mapo tofu, twice-cooked pork and fish-fragrant aubergines (Dunlop, via SBS, NPR/VPM and The Caterer).
- **Practical tip:** Fry it gently in oil at the start, before adding liquids, until the oil reddens and smells fragrant (SBS; The Caterer). Labels vary: The Woks of Life says the spicy kind is marked 辣 ("spicy"), and some jars labelled "doubanjiang" are non-spicy or sweet bean pastes.
- **Cultural context:** Dunlop calls mapo tofu, made with "Chengdu's famous Pixian chilli bean paste", "a classic of Sichuan cookery" (SBS).
- **Food safety:** None beyond storage. Refrigerate after opening, move leftovers from packets into a clean jar, and use clean utensils; it keeps "for months" (The Woks of Life). It's high in salt.
- **Citations:**
  - La Doubanjiang (Spicy Bean Sauce), The Woks of Life. https://thewoksoflife.com/doubanjiang/
  - Fuchsia Dunlop's mapo tofu, SBS Food. https://www.sbs.com.au/food/recipe/fuchsia-dunlops-mapo-tofu/fwvcw6ya9
  - Twice-Cooked Pork (Dunlop), NPR via VPM. https://www.vpm.org/npr-news/2008-05-02/twice-cooked-pork
  - Fish-fragrant aubergines, by Fuchsia Dunlop, The Caterer. https://www.thecaterer.com/news/fish-fragrant-aubergines-by-fuschia-dunlop
  - The National (Choudhury, 2020), URL as above.

### black-vinegar (Chinkiang / Zhenjiang)

- **Suggested category:** vinegar / condiment
- **Slot types:** acid
- **Cuisines:** sichuan. Dunlop uses it in fish-fragrant aubergines, gong bao chicken, cold-dressed chicken and cold buckwheat noodles. It comes from Zhenjiang in Jiangsu, a region that isn't in our Cuisine list.
  - Cantonese use (for example Jingdu / Zhenjiang spare ribs) is unverified; see section 5.
- **Factual points (essay):**
  1. It's made from fermented black sticky rice or glutinous rice, sometimes with sorghum or wheat, and it comes from Zhenjiang in Jiangsu province (The Woks of Life).
  2. It's dark, malty and complex, milder in acidity than distilled white vinegar, and faintly sweet (The Woks of Life).
  3. In Sichuan cooking it supplies the sour half of sweet-sour sauces. Dunlop's gong bao chicken has a "gentle sweet-sour sauce" made with Chinkiang vinegar (Splendid Table), and fish-fragrant sauce includes "a little sweet and sour flavouring" (The Caterer).
  4. It's also a classic dip for soup dumplings, often with julienned ginger (The Woks of Life). That's a Jiangsu/Shanghai use, outside our Cuisines.
- **Practical tip:** Stir it in near the end. In Dunlop's fish-fragrant aubergines it goes in with the spring onions just before the dish comes off the heat (The Caterer). In a pinch, swap in rice vinegar or a little balsamic, though some balsamics are very sweet (The Woks of Life).
- **Cultural context:** Look for yellow labels marked "Chinkiang Vinegar" or "Zhenjiang Vinegar" (The Woks of Life).
- **Food safety:** None. Store it in a cool, dry place (The Woks of Life).
- **Citations:**
  - Chinese Black Vinegar, The Woks of Life. https://thewoksoflife.com/chinese-black-vinegar/
  - Gong Bao Chicken with Peanuts (Fuchsia Dunlop, from *Every Grain of Rice*), The Splendid Table. https://www.splendidtable.org/story/2015/02/13/gong-bao-chicken-with-peanuts
  - Fish-fragrant aubergines, by Fuchsia Dunlop, The Caterer, URL as above.
  - Fuchsia Dunlop's Cold-dressed Chicken, Sous Chef. https://www.souschef.co.uk/blogs/the-bureau-of-taste/cold-dressed-chicken

### rice-vinegar

- **Suggested category:** vinegar / condiment
- **Slot types:** acid
- **Cuisines:**
  - japanese: MAFF komezu page; Just One Cookbook.
  - cantonese: Tony Tan quoting chef Tsang Chiu Lit of Ming Court.
- **Factual points (essay):**
  1. Japanese *komezu* is made "using only rice and water" (米酢は米、水のみを原料としてつくられる). Kyoto's traditional static fermentation takes half a year to a year (MAFF, translated).
  2. MAFF describes it as having "little sourness or harshness, with a mellow taste and pleasant aroma" (酸味やえぐみが少なく、まろやかで香りがよい).
  3. Its uses include sushi rice, vinegared dishes such as namasu, seafood marinades and dressings (MAFF).
  4. In Cantonese sweet and sour pork, Tony Tan reports Ming Court's Tsang Chiu Lit's view that "the secret to a great sauce is to use only rice vinegar, for its low acidity". The recipe specifies "rice vinegar (white not black)."
- **Practical tip:** Recipes call for unseasoned rice vinegar (for example Just One Cookbook's nanbanzuke), so check the label: "seasoned" rice vinegar already has sugar and salt in it.
- **Cultural context:** "In Kyoto, vinegar has long been treated as an indispensable seasoning." MAFF also cites the Edo-period text *Honchō Shokukan*, which rated vinegar from Kyoto and Fushimi the best.
- **Food safety:** None.
- **Citations:**
  - 米酢（こめず）, にっぽん伝統食図鑑, MAFF (Japanese). https://www.maff.go.jp/j/keikaku/syokubunka/traditional-foods/menu/komezu.html
  - Soy sauce, miso, other seasonings, Traditional Foods in Japan, MAFF. https://www.maff.go.jp/e/policies/market/dento_syoku/bunrui/syouyu-miso.html
  - Sweet and sour pork, Tony Tan, Gourmet Traveller, URL as above.
  - Salmon Nanbanzuke, Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook. https://www.justonecookbook.com/salmon-nanbanzuke/

### shiso

- **Suggested category:** herb
- **Slot types:** soft herb, garnish
- **Cuisines:** japanese. Source: Just One Cookbook (shiso guide).
- **Factual points (essay):**
  1. "Shiso is a member of the mint family"; it's also called perilla or ooba (Just One Cookbook).
  2. "Green shiso leaves are more common by far and are used all year round in Japanese cooking." Red shiso is used mainly to colour umeboshi and pickles.
  3. The flavour is "vibrant, herbaceous, and citrusy with a mildly astringent taste and bitter finish."
  4. It "frequently makes appearances in sushi dishes and sashimi plates." Whole leaves are fried as tempura.
- **Practical tip:** Cover the leaves with a damp paper towel and keep them in an airtight bag in the crisper drawer; use them soon (Just One Cookbook). Their aroma fades with storage, so add them fresh.
- **Cultural context:** Just One Cookbook says Korean perilla (kkaennip) is a different plant with a bolder, peppery flavour and advises against using it in place of shiso. Other Just One Cookbook pages are more lenient, so this varies.
- **Food safety:** For shiso-garlic soy sauce, keep it in the fridge (up to 2 months), dry the leaves fully first, and use clean utensils (Just One Cookbook).
- **Citations:**
  - What Is Shiso And How Is It Used?, Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook. https://www.justonecookbook.com/shiso-perilla/
  - Shiso Garlic Soy Sauce, Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook. https://www.justonecookbook.com/shiso-garlic-soy-sauce/

### salmon

- **Suggested category:** fish
- **Slot types:** protein
- **Cuisines:** japanese. Sources: MAFF (Ishikari nabe); Just One Cookbook (miso salmon, nanbanzuke, onigiri).
- **Factual points (essay):**
  1. Ishikari nabe is "a fisherman's dish from Ishikari Town, located at the mouth of the Ishikari River, famous for salmon": salmon and roe in kelp broth with vegetables, "seasoned with miso" (MAFF).
  2. Salmon fishing in the Ishikari region dates to the Edo period, and the dish became nationally known around 1950 (MAFF).
  3. In home cooking, salmon is often marinated in miso and broiled. Just One Cookbook says salmon works well in the saikyo-yaki style, named for Kyoto's sweet white miso.
  4. Its fattiness suits sharp or herbal partners. Just One Cookbook suggests pairing shiso "with fatty fish like salmon."
- **Practical tip:** Wipe off the miso marinade before broiling, because "miso burns easily." Don't marinate overnight, because the marinade "is very salty" (Just One Cookbook).
- **Cultural context:** Salmon is a common onigiri filling, and flaked leftover salmon goes into rice balls (Just One Cookbook).
- **Food safety:** Cook to 145°F / 63°C, or until the flesh "is clear and separates easily with a fork." Raw fish is safest if it has been previously frozen, since freezing kills parasites. Pregnant women, children, older adults and people with weakened immune systems should avoid raw or undercooked seafood (FDA).
- **Citations:**
  - Ishikarinabe (Ishikari hot pot), MAFF, URL as above.
  - Miso Salmon, Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook. https://www.justonecookbook.com/miso-salmon/
  - Miso Cod (Black Cod with Miso), Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook. https://www.justonecookbook.com/black-cod-with-miso/
  - Selecting and Serving Fresh and Frozen Seafood Safely, FDA. https://www.fda.gov/food/buy-store-serve-safe-food/selecting-and-serving-fresh-and-frozen-seafood-safely

### miso

- **Suggested category:** condiment (fermented paste)
- **Slot types:** none fit cleanly. Its closest role is a seasoning base (aromatic); see section 5.
- **Cuisines:** japanese. Sources: MAFF; Just One Cookbook.
- **Factual points (essay):**
  1. MAFF says it's made by mixing rice koji and salt into steamed soybeans, and that "Soybean protein is decomposed by proteolytic enzymes from Aspergillus oryzae into amino acids, which gives rich umami to miso."
  2. Ageing ranges "from several days to two years, which gives different flavor and taste" (MAFF). White miso is "light, sweet, and smooth." Red miso is long-fermented, darker and saltier (Just One Cookbook).
  3. Miso varies by region: MAFF lists salty Tsugaru miso, sweet Saikyo miso, red-brown Hatcho miso of Nagoya and Kyushu barley miso. Kyoto white miso is used in zoni, dengaku and miso-zuke (MAFF, translated).
  4. "Fermented seasoning such as miso, fermented soy bean, and soy sauce can be considered as the key to the taste of Japanese cuisine" (MAFF).
- **Practical tip:** Add miso to soup off the boil: "Avoid a full boil, which diminishes miso's delicate aroma" (Just One Cookbook, tonjiru).
- **Cultural context:** "Every household makes tonjiru a little differently" (Just One Cookbook). Choosing which miso to use is a household and regional matter.
- **Food safety:** Refrigerate after opening (Just One Cookbook). MAFF notes that low-salt white miso keeps less well than other miso. It's high in sodium.
- **Citations:**
  - Soy sauce, miso, other seasonings, Traditional Foods in Japan, MAFF, URL as above.
  - Diversity of WASHOKU, MAFF. https://www.maff.go.jp/e/policies/market/divers.html
  - 白味噌, にっぽん伝統食図鑑, MAFF (Japanese). https://www.maff.go.jp/j/keikaku/syokubunka/traditional-foods/menu/siromiso.html
  - Miso Guide, Namiko Hirasawa Chen, Just One Cookbook. https://www.justonecookbook.com/miso/
  - Tonjiru, Just One Cookbook, URL as above.

## 3. Proposed Pairings (35)

The pairs below don't appear in `pairings.yaml`. "Weak" marks a pairing where the source shows the two only as co-ingredients or as an optional garnish.

### Sichuan

1. **sichuan-pepper + chile** (tradition). Together they make mala, the numbing-and-hot taste of much Sichuan cooking. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
   - The Woks of Life, Sichuan Peppercorns: paired with chilies they create "a flavor known as mala", numbing-spicy.
   - The National: "Combined in a dish, the chilli and the pepper make for one of the most assertive notes of Sichuanese cuisine: the style known locally as 'mala', or numbing and hot."
   - Splendid Table (Dunlop's gong bao): "its kick of scorched chilli, tingle of Sichuan pepper."
2. **sichuan-pepper + chicken-thigh** (tradition). In Chongqing chicken (la zi ji), fried pieces of thigh are tossed with whole Sichuan pepper and a heap of dried chillies. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
   - The Woks of Life, Chongqing Chicken (Judy Leung): 3 boneless thighs and 2 tbsp Sichuan peppercorns. "I chose to use boneless chicken thighs instead."
   - Caveats: the thigh is that author's choice, and Dunlop's gong bao uses breast. Chongqing has been a separate municipality since 1997, though its cooking is generally grouped with Sichuan.
3. **sichuan-pepper + doubanjiang** (tradition). Mapo tofu is built on chilli bean paste and finished with ground Sichuan pepper. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
   - SBS (Dunlop's mapo tofu): "fry the chilli bean sauce until the oil turns red… serve scattered with ground Sichuan pepper."
4. **sichuan-pepper + black-vinegar** (tradition). Sichuan cold dressings often combine black vinegar's malty sourness with ground roasted Sichuan pepper. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
   - Sous Chef (Dunlop, cold-dressed chicken): the sauce includes Chinkiang vinegar plus ground roasted pepper or Sichuan pepper oil.
   - Irish News (Dunlop's cold buckwheat noodles): "2tbsp Chinkiang vinegar… 0.25-0.5tsp ground roasted Sichuan pepper (optional)."
   - Sous Chef also notes: "There is no master recipe for cold-dressed chicken: every cook makes it differently."
5. **doubanjiang + pork** (tradition). In twice-cooked pork, boiled sliced pork is stir-fried with chilli bean paste. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
   - NPR/VPM (Dunlop): "Push the pork to one side of the wok and tip the chili bean paste into the space you have created."
   - The Woks of Life (doubanjiang) also lists Twice Cooked Pork among its uses.
6. **doubanjiang + eggplant** (tradition). In fish-fragrant aubergines, chilli bean paste fried in oil makes the sauce that soft fried aubergine soaks up. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
   - The Caterer (Dunlop): "Stir-fry the chilli bean paste until the oil turns red and fragrant", after which the aubergines simmer in the sauce. The National says the dish gets its colour from "pickled chillies combined with broad beans in chilli bean paste."
7. **doubanjiang + garlic** (aromatic bridge). Garlic usually goes into the oil straight after the chilli bean paste, deepening the base. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
   - The Caterer (Dunlop): fry the paste "then add the ginger and garlic"; 3 tsp of each, chopped.
   - SBS (mapo tofu): "4 garlic cloves, minced."
8. **doubanjiang + ginger** (aromatic bridge). Ginger is fried in the reddened bean-paste oil alongside garlic. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
   - The Caterer (Dunlop), as above.
   - SBS: "1 tbsp grated ginger" in mapo tofu.
9. **doubanjiang + scallion** (tradition). Spring onions simmer in chilli bean sauce in mapo tofu, and green tops finish fish-fragrant dishes. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
   - SBS (Dunlop): "Add the fried beef and spring onion and simmer for 5 minutes or until the spring onion is softened."
   - The Caterer: spring onions, "green parts only, sliced into fine rings", go in at the end.
10. **doubanjiang + black-vinegar** (contrast). Black vinegar's soft sourness balances the salty chilli bean paste in fish-fragrant sauce. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
    - The Caterer (Dunlop): the ingredients include Sichuanese chilli bean paste and "1½ tsp Chinkiang or Chinese black vinegar." "Fish-fragrant" is described as "one of the classic flavour combinations of Sichuanese cookery", with "a little sweet and sour flavouring."
11. **black-vinegar + eggplant** (contrast). A splash of black vinegar at the end sharpens rich, oil-soft aubergine. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
    - The Caterer (Dunlop): "Stir in the vinegar and spring onions briefly, then remove from the heat."
12. **black-vinegar + chile** (contrast). In Sichuan dressings and gong bao sauce, black vinegar's sourness pairs with chilli heat. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
    - Irish News (Dunlop's cold noodles): "2tbsp Chinkiang vinegar", "2tbsp chilli oil, plus 1 tbsp sediment."
    - Splendid Table (gong bao): "a good handful of dried chillies" and "3 teaspoons Chinkiang or black Chinese vinegar."
13. **black-vinegar + garlic** (tradition). Both go into fish-fragrant sauce: garlic fried at the start, vinegar stirred in at the end. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
    - Irish News (Dunlop, fish-fragrant aubergines): "1.5tbsp finely chopped garlic", "1tbsp Chinkiang vinegar."
    - The Caterer, as above.
    - Weak-to-moderate: the source shows them as co-ingredients in one flavour profile.
14. **eggplant + garlic** (tradition). Garlic is one of the defining seasonings of Sichuan fish-fragrant aubergine. *Cuisines:* sichuan.
    - The Caterer: fish-fragrant is named for the seasonings of traditional fish cookery, "pickled chillies, ginger, garlic, and spring onions."

### Japanese

15. **eggplant + ginger** (tradition). Grilled, peeled eggplant (yaki nasu) is usually eaten with grated ginger, bonito flakes and soy sauce. *Cuisines:* japanese, sichuan.
    - RecipeTin Japan (Yumiko): "The perfectly grilled eggplant is traditionally eaten with ginger, bonito flakes and some soy sauce."
    - For Sichuan, ginger is among the fish-fragrant seasonings (The Caterer).
16. **miso + eggplant** (tradition). Nasu dengaku is eggplant grilled with a sweet miso glaze. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, Miso Glazed Eggplant: adapts "the classic dish nasu no miso dengaku." Tip: "Don't overload your eggplant with sweet miso glaze or it will taste too salty."
    - MAFF (白味噌) lists dengaku among the dishes that use Kyoto white miso.
17. **miso + salmon** (tradition). Salmon is simmered in miso hot pot (Ishikari nabe) or marinated in miso and broiled. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - MAFF: "chopped salmon meat and fish roe in a kelp broth with vegetables, seasoned with miso."
    - Just One Cookbook: "sea bass and salmon also work well with this miso marinade."
18. **miso + pork** (tradition). Tonjiru is pork and vegetables simmered in a miso soup. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook: "Pork belly, dashi, and miso work together to give this vegetable-packed soup a rich, deeply satisfying broth."
19. **miso + scallion** (tradition). Sliced green onion is the usual finish for miso soups such as tonjiru. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook (tonjiru): "garnish with green onion, and serve immediately."
    - Just One Cookbook (miso salmon) also tops the fish with green onion.
20. **miso + ginger** (aromatic bridge). Ginger added at the end of a miso soup brings a fresh, peppery bite. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook (tonjiru): "Adding it at the end preserves its fresh aroma and peppery bite."
21. **miso + rice-vinegar** (tradition). Mixed with sugar, they make sumiso, a sweet-sour dressing for vegetables and seafood. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, Asparagus with Miso Dressing: "The sauce is typically made with miso, rice vinegar, and sugar." Leeks, wakame, cucumber, octopus and squid are listed as other classic partners.
22. **pork + ginger** (tradition). In shogayaki, a common Japanese home dish, thin pork slices are pan-fried in a ginger-soy sauce. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook: "Ginger Pork, or what we call Shogayaki (生姜焼き), is a homey Japanese dish." The sauce includes "juice from the grated ginger."
23. **pork + shiso** (tradition). Thin pork is often rolled or served with shiso leaves in Japanese home cooking. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, Ginger Pork Rolls with Eggplant: "It's a favorite in Japanese home-style cooking. You'll love the combination of thinly sliced pork, eggplant, and shiso leaves."
24. **pork + eggplant** (tradition). Thin pork wrapped around eggplant and glazed with ginger soy is a home-style Japanese combination. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, same page: "One of my favorite flavor combinations includes pork, eggplant, shiso leaves, and a ginger-based sweet soy sauce."
25. **shiso + eggplant** (contrast). Fresh shiso lifts soft, rich eggplant, either rolled in with it or scattered over miso-glazed eggplant. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, Ginger Pork Rolls with Eggplant, as above.
    - Just One Cookbook, Miso Glazed Eggplant: "Sprinkle with optional sesame seeds, sliced green onions, and julienned shiso leaves."
    - Weak-to-moderate.
26. **shiso + salmon** (contrast). Shiso's citrusy, herbal edge suits fatty salmon, as in salmon onigiri. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, Shiso guide: "Pair with fatty fish like salmon, yellowtail, and tuna or ikura (salmon roe)."
    - Just One Cookbook, Salmon and Shio Kombu Onigiri: shiso and myoga add "fragrant and refreshing balance."
27. **shiso + garlic** (tradition). Shiso leaves and sliced garlic steeped in soy sauce make a popular Japanese home condiment. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, Shiso Garlic Soy Sauce: "basically soy sauce infused with shiso and garlic"; "everyone in Japan was making shiso garlic soy sauce."
    - Food safety: keep it refrigerated.
28. **salmon + rice-vinegar** (contrast). In nanbanzuke, fried salmon is marinated in a rice-vinegar dressing and served chilled. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, Salmon Nanbanzuke: the marinade uses "rice vinegar (unseasoned)." "The most important technique for delicious nanbanzuke is to marinate the fried salmon as soon as it's out of the oil."
29. **salmon + onion** (tradition). Onion goes into both Ishikari nabe and salmon nanbanzuke. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - MAFF: "Vegetables often include onions, cabbage, leeks, radish, shiitake mushrooms, tofu."
    - Just One Cookbook (nanbanzuke): the onion is thinly sliced, sautéed and laid over the fried salmon.
30. **rice-vinegar + onion** (tradition). Thinly sliced onion is marinated in the rice-vinegar dressing of nanbanzuke, and it also goes into Cantonese sweet and sour pork. *Cuisines:* japanese, cantonese.
    - Just One Cookbook (nanbanzuke), as above.
    - Gourmet Traveller (Tony Tan): "1 small onion" alongside "1 tbsp rice vinegar (white not black)."
    - Weak for Cantonese.
31. **rice-vinegar + ginger** (tradition). Gari, the pickled ginger served with sushi, is young ginger pickled in sweetened rice vinegar. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, Pickled Ginger: "very thin slices of young ginger soaked in sweet vinegar brine (amazu)"; "The spiciness and sweet vinegar flavor serve as a palate cleanser."
32. **salmon + scallion** (contrast). Weak. Sliced green onion is an optional finish on miso-broiled salmon. *Cuisines:* japanese.
    - Just One Cookbook, Miso Salmon: "Top the salmon with… 1 green onion/scallion thinly sliced (both optional)."
    - I'd drop this one unless the graph wants it.

### Cantonese

33. **pork + rice-vinegar** (contrast). Cantonese sweet and sour pork balances fried pork with a rice-vinegar sauce. *Cuisines:* cantonese.
    - Gourmet Traveller (Tony Tan): "Sweet and sour pork is a classic Cantonese dish"; Tsang Chiu Lit (Ming Court): "the secret to a great sauce is to use only rice vinegar, for its low acidity."
34. **pork + garlic** (tradition). Minced garlic goes into the marinade for Cantonese char siu. *Cuisines:* cantonese.
    - The Woks of Life (Bill Leung), Char Siu: the marinade includes "3 cloves, finely minced" garlic, with hoisin, five spice, soy and Shaoxing wine.
    - Note that Sichuan twice-cooked pork uses green garlic or leeks, not garlic cloves (NPR/VPM).
35. **pork + scallion** (tradition). Scallions can stand in for the leeks in Sichuan twice-cooked pork, and green onion finishes Japanese tonjiru. *Cuisines:* sichuan, japanese.
    - NPR/VPM (Dunlop): "Long, green Chinese scallions can also be used instead of leeks."
    - Just One Cookbook (tonjiru): "garnish with green onion."
    - Weak-to-moderate.

## 5. Couldn't verify, and open questions

- **Black vinegar in Cantonese cooking** (for example Jingdu / Zhenjiang spare ribs, 京都骨). I found only a family blog (Wok and Kin, which doesn't place the dish regionally), a sauce-maker's recipe and an unvetted blog, so I didn't tag it. pork + black-vinegar is left out for the same reason. Dunlop's fish-fragrant pork slivers would support it for Sichuan, but I only found reposts on blogs.
- **Cilantro in Sichuan cooking** (for example as a garnish on kou shui ji). Every source I found was a blog or recipe aggregator, and none of the Dunlop recipes I read use coriander. cilantro + chicken-thigh and chile + cilantro have no citation from my traditions.
- **sichuan-pepper + scallion** (the *jiao ma* pounded sauce) and **sichuan-pepper + cumin / lamb**: I didn't find a fetchable primary source, so they aren't proposed.
- **shiso + ginger, shiso + rice-vinegar, salmon + lemon**: plausible Japanese combinations (yakumi, sushi), but I didn't find a fetched source, so they aren't proposed.
- **Pages that failed:**
  - Saveur's mapo tofu article returned 403. It reportedly quotes Dunlop: "You can't say it's mapo tofu if you don't have chile bean paste and Sichuan pepper." Not cited.
  - The NPR transcript of the Dunlop interview timed out. Not cited.
  - The USDA FSIS temperature chart and foodsafety.gov returned 403, so CDC is used instead.
  - MAFF's second Ishikari nabe URL (`…/menu/ishikari_nabe_hokkaido.html`) returned 403. The `/565/index.html` version worked and is the one cited.
  - A sanshool study on PubMed was blocked by a cookie wall, so there's no academic citation for the numbing mechanism.
- **Source quality caveats:**
  - The Caterer was paywalled, so I could read only excerpts and a summary.
  - Sous Chef is a retailer's blog, though it reprints Dunlop with credit.
  - The Woks of Life's Chongqing chicken page says "Chongqing is located in the Sichuan province", which is outdated; Chongqing has been a separate municipality since 1997.
  - The Woks of Life black vinegar page contained text addressed to automated readers. I ignored it and used only the article's factual content.
- **Model gap:** miso and doubanjiang don't fit the slot types offered (protein, aromatic, acid, soft herb, heat, vegetable, garnish). A "seasoning" or "savoury base" slot type might be worth a decision before authoring.
- **Regional naming:** Zhenjiang vinegar comes from Jiangsu, and soup-dumpling dips with ginger and black vinegar are Jiangsu/Shanghai practice. Neither is in our Cuisine list, so they aren't tagged.
